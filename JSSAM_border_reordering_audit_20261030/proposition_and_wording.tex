\begin{proposition}
Let $0<\pi_i\leq1$ and $\sum_{i=1}^N\pi_i=n\in\mathbb{N}$.
Arrange the original units in any permutation as consecutive half-open
intervals of lengths $\pi_i$ on $[0,n)$. For $U\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1)$,
select the units whose intervals contain $U,U+1,\ldots,U+n-1$.
Then the sample contains exactly $n$ distinct units almost surely, and
$\Pr(i\in S)=\pi_i$. Subdividing a unit interval into adjacent pieces does
not change the design. Equal-mass exchanges of distinct units between
two samples preserve their sizes and the first-order inclusion probabilities.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Each point belongs to exactly one interval. An interval of length at
most one contains at most one of these points, except on a null set of
endpoints. Its projection modulo one has length $\pi_i$, giving its
selection probability. For an exchange of mass $\delta$ between samples
$A$ and $B$, with $a\in A\setminus B$ and $b\in B\setminus A$, move mass
$0\leq\delta\leq\min\{p(A),p(B)\}$ to $A-a+b$ and $B-b+a$. Each unit's net
inclusion mass is unchanged, as are the sample sizes and total probability.
\end{proof}
